Dogs and Frozen Lakes: Ice Safety

Every winter, dogs fall through ice, and people die trying to rescue them. How to prevent it, and what to do if it happens.

2 min read · Updated November 2026

Frozen ponds and lakes look solid and tempting. But ice thickness varies, especially near shorelines, inlets, outlets, docks and anywhere water moves. A dog chasing a ball or a bird can run onto thin ice in seconds.

Prevention

  • Keep your dog leashed near frozen water, unless the area is clearly away from the ice.
  • Never throw toys onto the ice.
  • Teach a rock-solid recall and "leave it" long before winter.
  • Know the rule of thumb: official guidance typically calls for at least 4 inches of clear, solid ice for a person on foot. You can't judge that by looking, and ice near shore and moving water is often thinner.
  • Stay off ice early and late in the season, and whenever it's slushy, cracked or darker in color.

If your dog falls through

Don't go on the ice after your dog. Many people who drown in winter were trying to rescue a pet. Dogs are often able to get out on their own, or can be helped from shore.

  1. Call 911 immediately. Firefighters have ice rescue training and gear.
  2. Stay on shore and call your dog encouragingly toward you, where the ice is likely thicker.
  3. Reach, don't go. If you can safely reach with a long branch, pole, rope or leash from shore, do so.
  4. Once out, wrap your dog in a blanket or coat, get them somewhere warm and head to a vet. Cold water can cause hypothermia and breathing problems from inhaled water.

Signs of hypothermia

Intense shivering (which may stop as it worsens), weakness, slow breathing, stiff muscles, pale gums and confusion. Warm your dog gradually with blankets and warm (not hot) water bottles wrapped in towels, and get to a vet.

This guide is general information, not veterinary advice. If your dog is sick, hurt or acting unusually, call your veterinarian.
