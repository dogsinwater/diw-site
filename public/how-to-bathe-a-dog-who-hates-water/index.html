When Your Dog Hates Water: Baths, Fear and Building Confidence

Plenty of dogs are afraid of water. How to make bath time easier, and gently build confidence if you'd like your dog to enjoy more water.

2 min read · Updated November 2026

Some dogs hate water: Chihuahuas, many terriers, sighthounds and plenty of mixed breeds. Fear often comes from a bad experience, slippery surfaces, cold water or simply never being introduced to it gently.

Your dog doesn't need to swim. But a calm bath and comfortable trips near water make life easier for both of you.

Easier baths

  • Prepare everything first: shampoo, cup, towels and treats within reach.
  • Use lukewarm water. Test it on your wrist.
  • Put down a non-slip mat. Slipping is a big part of the fear. Non-slip bath mats
  • Use a cup or a gentle sprayer, not a strong blast. Start at the paws and work up. Keep water out of the eyes and ears.
  • Distract with a lick mat. Spread a little peanut butter (check it's xylitol-free) or plain yogurt on a lick mat stuck to the tub wall. Lick mats
  • Talk calmly and keep it short.
  • Towel well, with lots of praise and a treat at the end.
  • Between baths, use grooming wipes or waterless shampoo to cut down how often you need a full bath.

Building confidence step by step

The goal is to change how your dog feels about water, slowly enough that they never panic.

  1. Start dry. Reward your dog just for stepping into an empty tub or kiddie pool.
  2. Add an inch of water. Reward calm steps in and out.
  3. Increase gradually over days or weeks, one small step at a time.
  4. Let your dog choose. Never lift or push them in. If they back off, go back a step.
  5. Pair water with the best things: favorite treats, toys and games only happen near the water.

For outdoor water, use the same approach at a calm, shallow lake edge with a life jacket on. Some dogs come to love wading; some will only ever paddle. Both are wins.

If fear is intense, ask your vet or a reward-based trainer for help.

This guide is general information, not veterinary advice. If your dog is sick, hurt or acting unusually, call your veterinarian.
