Calm water and moving water are different worlds. A dog who swims happily in a pond can be overpowered by a river current or a rip current in seconds.

## Rivers and streams

- **Look before you let your dog in.** Fast, smooth water often moves faster than it looks. Whitewater, eddies, fallen trees ("strainers") and low dams are especially dangerous.
- **Choose slow, shallow spots** with an easy, gradual exit on your side.
- **Throw toys upstream,** not downstream, so the current carries your dog back toward you.
- **After rain, stay out.** Rivers rise fast and carry debris.
- **Watch for cold water.** Snowmelt-fed rivers can be cold enough to cause cramping and exhaustion even on warm days.

## Ocean waves and rip currents

A rip current is a narrow channel of water moving away from shore. Look for a gap in breaking waves, darker or choppier water, or foam and debris moving out to sea.

- Keep your dog out of the water near posted rip current warnings.
- Avoid throwing toys past the breaking waves.
- Use a life jacket in surf.

## If your dog is swept away

This is the hardest advice to follow, but it saves lives: **don't go in after your dog.** Every year, people drown trying to rescue dogs, and many of those dogs make it out on their own.

Instead:

1. **Call your dog and move along the bank or beach** to where they're heading, and toward an easy exit.
2. **Encourage them toward shore**, in a rip current, by moving sideways along the beach so they swim parallel to shore.
3. **Use something that floats or reaches:** a pole, a branch, a rope or a throwable flotation device.
4. **Call 911** or alert a lifeguard if your dog is in real trouble.

After any scare in the water, watch your dog closely and see a vet if they cough, struggle to breathe, or seem weak or unusually tired. Inhaled water can cause problems hours later.
