Many dogs love boating: the breeze, the smells, the swimming stops. Getting there takes a bit of preparation.

## Before the first trip

- **Introduce the boat at the dock.** Let your dog explore it while it's tied up and the engine's off, with treats and praise. Then try running the engine at the dock, then a short, slow ride.
- **Practice the life jacket** at home first. See [life jackets](/doggy-life-jackets/).
- **Teach "stay" and "settle"** on a mat, so your dog has a job while the boat is moving.
- **Plan a way back aboard.** Even with a handle, lifting a wet 70-pound dog is hard. See [boat ramps and ladders](/the-right-dog-ramp-for-boating/).
- **Check the rules.** Some marinas, parks and charter boats limit dogs.

## Where your dog should ride

- **In the cockpit or on the floor,** low and toward the middle of the boat, not on the bow, the gunwales or a seat edge.
- **Never on the bow while moving.** A wave or a sudden turn can throw them overboard, and into the propeller's path.
- **Out of direct sun** and away from hot surfaces, engine covers and fishing gear.

## Seasickness

Signs include drooling, yawning, lip licking, restlessness and vomiting. Start with short trips on calm water, keep your dog where motion is least (low and near the middle), and skip a big meal before you go. If your dog gets seasick, ask your vet about anti-nausea medication you can give before trips.

## Swimming stops

**Turn the engine off before your dog goes in the water,** and keep it off until they're back aboard. Swim on the side away from currents and traffic, and keep your dog on a floating leash or within easy reach.

## Bathroom breaks

Plan for shore stops every few hours, or train your dog to use a turf pad on board. Many dogs won't go on a boat at first, so bring them ashore regularly on long days.
